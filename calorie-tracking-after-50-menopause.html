Calorie tracking after 50 and during menopause

Weight can behave differently in midlife. Tracking still helps if you use it to spot patterns instead of chasing one perfect number.

By Ali Gürkan Sevilmiş, developer of Pofu · November 7, 2026

The short answer

Calorie tracking can work after 50, but expect it to be less predictable than it was at 30. A 2012 review in Climacteric found that weight gain itself cannot be attributed to menopause, although the hormonal change is linked to more body fat, especially around the abdomen. Use calories as one signal next to strength training, sleep and how you feel, and talk to your doctor about symptoms.

What changes around menopause

The NHS describes menopause as usually starting between 45 and 55, and says symptoms differ a lot: some people have a big impact on daily life, others hardly notice any. That variety is the first thing to accept. A rule that worked for a friend may not work for you.

On weight, the evidence is more careful than most headlines. The International Menopause Society review concluded that weight gain per se cannot be pinned on the menopause transition, but that the hormonal shift goes along with a rise in total body fat and abdominal fat. Age, activity, sleep and mood all move at the same time, so it is hard to separate causes, for you and for researchers.

What to track, and what to leave alone

  • Calories as a range. The NHS gives 2,000 kcal a day as a guide for an average woman and says needs depend on age, weight, height and activity. Treat any calculator, including ours, as a starting estimate and adjust after a few weeks of real data.
  • Protein and regular meals. They make a day easier to structure. Ask your doctor or a dietitian how much protein suits you, particularly if you have kidney or other conditions.
  • Weekly weight average. One weigh-in is noise. Use a weekly average and look at four-week trends.
  • Movement you can keep up. The NHS suggests regular exercise with a focus on weight-bearing activity, which helps protect bones.
  • Sleep and mood notes. The NHS lists regular sleep routines among the things that can help symptoms. A short note ("slept badly, ate more") often explains a week better than the calorie total.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting too hard. Very low targets make fatigue, poor sleep and low mood worse and are hard to hold. Our calculators never go below a 1,200 kcal floor, and for many people a gentler target is a better idea. Your doctor can help set one.
  • Using only the scale. If you are strength training, the scale can stay flat while your body changes.
  • Trusting cycle-phase estimates during perimenopause. If your periods have become irregular, phase-based adjustments are guesses. Look at trends over weeks.
  • Assuming it is "just hormones". Sudden weight change, new fatigue or swelling deserve a check-up.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Pick a calorie range with your doctor or from a calculator, and keep it for four weeks.
  2. Weigh in on the same few mornings a week and use the average.
  3. Add two short strength sessions or whatever weight-bearing activity you enjoy, if your doctor agrees.
  4. On Sunday, read the week: average intake, average weight, sleep, energy. Change one thing at most.

When to talk to a doctor

See a doctor if symptoms such as hot flushes, low mood, heavy or changed bleeding or poor sleep affect your daily life, if your weight changes quickly without a reason, or before you start a restrictive plan. Treatment decisions, including hormone therapy, belong with your doctor. Pofu cannot advise on them.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need fewer calories after menopause?

There is no single number. The NHS gives 2,000 kcal a day as a guide for an average woman and says needs vary with age, weight, height and activity. Use a calculator as a starting point and adjust with your doctor or a dietitian.

Is weight gain at menopause inevitable?

No. A 2012 review found weight gain per se cannot be attributed to menopause, though hormonal change is associated with more total and abdominal fat. Activity, sleep and food habits still matter.

Should my calorie target change during perimenopause?

Not automatically. If your cycle is irregular, phase-based adjustments are unreliable. Keep one steady target and judge it by multi-week trends.

Is intermittent fasting a good idea after 50?

It suits some people and not others. If you take medication, have diabetes or a history of disordered eating, ask your doctor first. If you try it, keep the window gentle.

Does Pofu have menopause features?

No. Pofu has no menopause-specific features. It is a general calorie counter with an optional cycle layer, designed for women.
