Calorie counting for beginners (women)

You do not need a perfect plan to start. You need a rough target, a logging habit and two weeks of honest data.

By Ali Gürkan Sevilmiş, developer of Pofu · November 7, 2026

The short answer

Start by logging what you eat for one week without changing anything, then pick a modest target from a calculator and adjust after two to four weeks of data. The NHS gives 2,000 kcal a day as a guide for an average woman, but your own number depends on age, weight, height and activity.

What calories actually are

A calorie (kcal on labels) is a unit of energy. The NHS explains that food and drink give you energy, and that you gain weight when you regularly take in more than you use. It also stresses that labels and estimates are guides. They are not exact, and nobody burns the same amount from the same food every day.

So counting is a way to notice patterns, not a way to prove you are "good" or "bad". Keep that framing from the beginning and the habit is much easier to hold.

Week one: observe first

  1. Pick one app and stay with it for at least two weeks. Switching apps resets your habits and your saved foods.
  2. Log everything you eat and drink without trying to change it. Include oil, sauces, milk in coffee and drinks, which beginners often forget.
  3. Do not eat less yet. The first week tells you your baseline.
  4. Weigh yourself a few mornings if you want, and use the average. Skip the scale if it makes you anxious. Photos, how clothes fit and energy are valid measures too.

Picking a starting target

Calculators (ours uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation and never goes below 1,200 kcal) give a starting estimate. Compare it with your week-one average. If you maintained your weight on your baseline, a modest reduction is a reasonable starting point if weight loss is a goal your doctor agrees with. Big cuts tend to be hard to maintain.

If you are under 18, pregnant, breastfeeding, have diabetes, or have ever had an eating disorder, talk to a doctor or dietitian before setting a target. The NHS weight loss plan is a free starting point for adults who want structure.

How to log without going mad

  • Use labels and barcodes for packaged food. They are the most reliable entries.
  • Estimate portions honestly but do not agonise. A kitchen scale helps for a week or two to calibrate your eye; you may not need it forever.
  • Prefer entries with plenty of sources. User-added foods in big databases can be wrong. If two entries for the same food differ a lot, pick the one that matches the label.
  • Photo logging is quick but approximate. Check the portion and the cooking oil before accepting.
  • Log before dinner if you can. Seeing what is left in the day is more useful than a verdict afterward.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Setting a very low target on day one, then quitting by Thursday.
  • Reacting to one day's weight. Daily weight moves with water, salt and digestion.
  • Counting only "bad" foods or only meals. Drinks and snacks add up.
  • Treating exercise calories from a watch as an exact bonus to eat back.
  • Giving up after a high day. One day moves a weekly average very little.
  • Letting numbers replace hunger, energy and enjoyment. If food starts to feel stressful, stop tracking and see a doctor if it continues.

Frequently asked questions

How many calories should a woman eat a day?

The NHS gives about 2,000 kcal a day as a guide for an average woman, but the right number depends on age, weight, height and activity. Start from a calculator, then check against two to four weeks of your own data.

Do I have to weigh my food?

No. Weighing for a week or two can calibrate your eye, but many people use labels, barcodes and portion estimates. Accept that results will be approximate.

How long before I see results?

It varies. Judge by multi-week trends, not days. Daily weight moves with water, salt and digestion.

Is photo logging accurate?

It is an estimate. Check portions and hidden oil. Pofu shows a confidence score and remembers corrections, but you should still review it.

Is calorie counting safe for everyone?

No. If you have had an eating disorder, are pregnant or breastfeeding, have diabetes or are under 18, talk to a doctor or dietitian first.
