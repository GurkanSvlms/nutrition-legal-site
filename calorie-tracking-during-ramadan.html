Calorie tracking during Ramadan

Ramadan is not a diet month. If you track, use it to make sure you are eating and drinking enough in a short window, not to squeeze your numbers down.

By Ali Gürkan Sevilmiş, developer of Pofu · November 7, 2026

The short answer

If you fast, you can log suhoor, iftar and any evening snacks to see whether you are eating balanced meals and drinking enough, and you can skip aggressive calorie targets. If you have diabetes, take medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of disordered eating, talk to your doctor before fasting or tracking. Whether and how you fast is your decision, and your doctor can advise on health risks.

Decide what tracking is for

For many people Ramadan changes the whole day: when you eat, sleep and exercise. That makes it a bad month for strict targets, and a decent month for a light record. Useful goals include noticing whether you are skipping suhoor, whether iftar is very large, whether you are drinking enough water between sunset and dawn, and whether you have enough protein and fibre across the two main meals.

If tracking tends to make you anxious about food, pause it. You can come back after Ramadan.

Health first, especially with diabetes

Diabetes UK advises people with diabetes to check in with their healthcare team before deciding to fast, because it can affect how you manage your condition, and it says fasting may not be a safe option for some people. Please follow your own team on this rather than an app. The same applies if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take regular medication, are unwell, or have a history of an eating disorder.

Calorie tracking does not replace any of that advice, and an app cannot tell you whether you are safe to fast. Questions about what the fast requires of you religiously are for you and the person you trust to ask.

How to log a fasting day

  • Treat suhoor and iftar as your two main meals, plus a name for whatever you eat in between, such as a "late snack" or "tarawih snack". Naming them stops the day from becoming one blob.
  • Log water too. A water counter is more useful than a calorie counter in this month.
  • Take a photo or quick barcode when you break your fast. Large shared dishes are hard to estimate, so a rough entry is fine.
  • Do not set one stern daily number. Watch weekly averages and how you feel. Low energy, dizziness or headaches are reasons to rethink, and medical symptoms are reasons to stop and get advice.
  • Keep a note of sleep. Disrupted nights change appetite for many people.

Patterns that trip people up

  • The iftar rebound. Eating very fast after a long day can make portions hard to judge. Starting with water and a lighter first course is a common approach, and taking a short pause before the main meal may help you notice fullness.
  • Sugary and fried festive foods. They are part of the occasion for many families. You do not have to remove them; log them honestly and see how the week balances out.
  • Skipping suhoor to save calories. That tends to leave you with less energy. A balanced suhoor is better than none.
  • Using Ramadan to lose weight fast. Weight changes during Ramadan vary widely between people. Treat it as a month of observation.

Eating, drinking and general advice: the WHO healthy diet fact sheet and the NHS balanced diet pages are good starting points.

After Ramadan

Your routine will change again at Eid. Use your records to set a realistic target for the following weeks, not to punish the month. Return to your usual meal times gradually and review your average intake after two weeks.

Frequently asked questions

Should I count calories during Ramadan?

It is optional. Some people find a light record helpful for noticing balance and hydration, others prefer not to track. Skip it if it causes anxiety or a history of disordered eating is a concern.

Can I fast if I have diabetes?

Please ask your healthcare team. Diabetes UK advises checking with your team before deciding to fast, and notes it may not be safe for some people.

Is it better to set a low calorie target during Ramadan?

Usually no. Eating a balanced suhoor and iftar and drinking enough fluids matter more than a strict number. Aim to maintain energy and watch weekly averages.

Does Pofu have a Ramadan mode?

No. Pofu has no Ramadan mode, sunrise and sunset timing, or prayer times. It has preset fasting windows and water tracking.

Which calorie app is best for iftar?

It depends on how you log. A photo or barcode is quick at the table, and a large database helps with home-cooked dishes. Try one or two and keep the one you actually use.
