Calorie tracking after the holidays

Holidays, travel and festive weeks change how you eat. Getting back on track is a gentle restart, not a punishment.

The short answer

After a holiday, resume normal meals and logging right away, skip the crash diet, and give your weight a couple of weeks to settle before judging it. The best-known study found an average holiday weight gain of about 0.37 kg, smaller than people often assume, though it did not fully reverse in the following months. A steady return to routine works better than compensating.

What the research says

A prospective study in the New England Journal of Medicine followed 195 adults, weighing them four times, 6 to 8 weeks apart, across the year-end holiday season. Average weight rose by 0.37 kg during the holiday period, a significant but small increase, and by about 0.48 kg between September and March. The authors concluded that the average holiday weight gain is less than commonly asserted, but that because it was not reversed in spring or summer, it probably contributes to weight gain over adult life. People who reported more physical activity gained less.

That is one study of one group at one time, so treat it as context rather than a rule. Your own change may be larger or nothing at all. Part of what you see on the scale in the first days after a trip may be water, salt and digestion, not fat.

The first three days back

  1. Eat normally. Skipping meals or a very low day tends to be followed by overeating. Return to your usual meals.
  2. Drink water and get moving gently, by walking, for example. These help you feel like yourself again and do not need to be "making up" for anything.
  3. Restart logging simply. If a full diary feels like too much, log only dinner for a few days.
  4. Weigh with patience. The scale after a trip is noisy. Wait one to two weeks and use the average.

A gentle two-week restart

  • Week one: re-establish your regular meal pattern and log most meals roughly. Do not change your target yet.
  • Week two: compare your average intake with your target. If it is near target, you are fine; if you are well above, nudge by a small amount instead of cutting hard.
  • Clear the kitchen of leftover treats if they keep calling you, or move them out of sight. This is practical, not moral.
  • Plan the next occasion. The next holiday comes. Decide in advance whether you will log lightly, log fully or take a tracking break.

The NHS weight loss plan offers a free structured option for adults if you want one.

Common mistakes

  • Crash dieting. A very low target after a feast is how a cycle of restricting and overeating starts. Our calculators use a 1,200 kcal floor as a minimum, not a recommendation.
  • "Earning" or "burning off" food. Exercise is good for you, but treating it as punishment makes both harder.
  • Waiting for Monday, or January. Restart today at a small scale.
  • Judging the whole holiday by the scale. Water, salt and travel change the number quickly.
  • Dropping the habit entirely because the break felt like failure. Taking a break is not failing, and the NHS gives 2,000 kcal a day only as a guide for an average woman anyway.

If you notice guilt, fear of food or compulsive compensation, talk to a doctor. Those are signs to step back from tracking.

Frequently asked questions

How much weight do people gain over the holidays?

In one often-cited study of 195 adults, the average gain during the holiday period was about 0.37 kg. Individual results vary, and a trip can also change water weight.

Should I do a detox or crash diet after the holidays?

No. Very low intake tends to backfire, and it is not an approach I can recommend. Return to regular meals and a steady, moderate target.

When should I weigh myself after a trip?

Wait a week or two and use an average. Early readings are affected by water, salt and digestion.

Do I need to log everything when restarting?

No. Logging a few meals roughly is enough to rebuild the habit. You can add detail later.

What if I cannot stop thinking about food after the holidays?

Step back from tracking and talk to a doctor or dietitian, especially if you feel guilt or compulsion around eating.
